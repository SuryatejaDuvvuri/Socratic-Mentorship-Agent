# NSF Merit Review Criteria
SOURCE: NSF — Merit Review (nsf.gov/funding/merit-review) and PAPPG 24-1
TAGS: evaluation, novelty, motivation, broader-impacts, rubric

## Intellectual Merit (definition)
Intellectual Merit is the potential for the proposed project to advance knowledge and understanding within its own field or across different fields. A proposal demonstrates intellectual merit when it makes clear what new knowledge the work will produce and why that advance is significant. The core reviewer question is: "Will this research expand what we know in the field, and is the advance important?" Vague claims that a project is "novel" or "important" do not satisfy this — the proposal must name the specific knowledge gap and the specific contribution.

## Broader Impacts (definition)
Broader Impacts is the potential for the proposed project to benefit society and contribute to the achievement of specific, desired societal outcomes. The proposal must include an explicit statement describing who benefits and how. Examples NSF values: improved STEM education and educator development, broadening participation of underrepresented groups, increased public scientific literacy, improved well-being, development of a globally competitive STEM workforce, and partnerships between academia and industry. Broader impacts must be concrete and tied to planned activities, not generic aspirations.

## Review Element 1: Creative and Transformative Potential
Reviewers ask: "To what extent do the proposed activities suggest and explore creative, original, or potentially transformative concepts?" A strong proposal shows that the idea is not incremental rehashing of prior work — it identifies a genuinely open problem and proposes an approach that could change how the field thinks about it. Transformative potential is demonstrated by contrast with the limits of existing approaches, not by asserting novelty.

## Review Element 2: Sound Planning and Assessment
Reviewers ask: "Is the plan for carrying out the proposed activities well-reasoned, well-organized, and based on a sound rationale? Does the plan incorporate a mechanism to assess success?" Every strong proposal answers five questions explicitly: what you want to do, why you want to do it, how you plan to do it, how you will know if you succeed, and what benefits accrue if it works. The presence of a concrete success/assessment mechanism (metrics, baselines, evaluation design) is what separates a fundable plan from an idea.

## Review Element 3: Qualifications
Reviewers ask: "How well qualified is the individual, team, or organization to conduct the proposed activities?" The proposal should make the match between the required expertise and the proposer's background evident. For a student proposal, this maps to demonstrating command of the prerequisite concepts and prior literature.

## Review Element 4: Adequate Resources
Reviewers ask: "Are there adequate resources available (at the home organization or through collaborations) to carry out the proposed activities?" Feasibility depends on access to data, tooling, compute, participants, and time. A proposal that ignores resource constraints reads as naive.

## The Five Questions Every Project Description Must Answer
A fundable project description answers, in order: (1) What do you want to do? (state objectives and expected significance). (2) Why do you want to do it? (relationship to current knowledge and the specific gap). (3) How do you plan to do it? (general work plan, methods, procedures). (4) How will you know if you succeed? (evaluation design, metrics, baselines). (5) What benefits could accrue if the project succeeds? (broader impacts). If any of the five is missing or vague, the proposal is incomplete regardless of how polished the prose is.
