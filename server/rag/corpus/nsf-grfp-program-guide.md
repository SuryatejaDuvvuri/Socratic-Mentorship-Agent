# NSF GRFP Program Guide — Structure, Requirements, and Reviewer Criteria
SOURCE: NSF Graduate Research Fellowship Program (nsf.gov/grfp), NSF GRFP Program Solicitation NSF 23-605
TAGS: rubric, evaluation, broader-impacts, novelty, motivation, method, grfp

## GRFP Personal Statement vs Research Plan — Key Distinction
The GRFP has TWO separate documents. The Personal Statement (3 pages) covers the applicant's background, broader impacts of their career, and personal story. The Research Plan (2 pages) is what your research mentorship agent targets: it must contain the research idea, intellectual merit, broader impacts of the research, and feasibility. Both documents are reviewed against the same two NSF merit review criteria: Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts. The Research Plan must be self-contained — reviewers should not need to read the Personal Statement to evaluate the research.

## NSF GRFP Page Limit and Format Requirements
Research Plan: 2 pages maximum (not including references). Reviewers will not read past page 2. Font: 12-point or larger. Margins: 1 inch or larger. References do not count against the page limit but must be cited in the document. A proposal that exceeds the page limit is disqualified. This means every word must earn its place: no long preambles, no repetition, no throat-clearing.

## What Reviewers Are Actually Evaluating
GRFP reviewers score each proposal on a 5-point scale: Excellent, Very Good, Good, Fair, Poor. The score is holistic, not a checklist. However, reviewers are trained to look for evidence of these specific qualities:
INTELLECTUAL MERIT evidence: Does the applicant understand the current state of the field? Is the proposed work original and significant? Has the applicant identified a gap that is real (not already solved)? Is the approach sound and feasible? Would this advance knowledge in the field?
BROADER IMPACTS evidence: Will this benefit society beyond the immediate research? Is there a plan for mentoring, outreach, or education? Are underrepresented groups included? Is there a concrete plan, not just aspirations?
Reviewers are frustrated by: vague novelty claims ("new approach"), missing hypotheses, methods that describe a tool without a research question, and broader impacts that only list publications.

## The "So What" Test Applied by GRFP Reviewers
Every paragraph in an accepted GRFP proposal implicitly answers the "so what" question. Reviewers ask:
- Motivation: "So what if this problem is not solved?" (The answer must involve concrete cost or consequence.)
- Gap: "So what if this gap exists?" (The answer must explain why existing work is insufficient and why the gap matters.)
- Intellectual Merit: "So what if this hypothesis is confirmed?" (The answer must name a field-level advance.)
- Method: "So what if this approach works?" (The answer must explain why this approach and not an obvious alternative.)
Proposals that don't survive the "so what" test at each paragraph are rated lower on both criteria.

## Two-Page Constraint — What Must Be Included
Given the 2-page limit, successful GRFP CS proposals allocate space roughly as follows:
- Introduction / Motivation / Gap: ~0.4 pages (hook, problem, gap — no longer)
- Intellectual Merit section (labeled): ~0.5 pages (novelty, contribution type, field advance)
- Broader Impacts section (labeled): ~0.3 pages (global + local tiers, concrete activities)
- Research Plan / Method: ~0.5 pages (approach, steps, dataset, metrics, baseline)
- Evaluation and Feasibility: ~0.2 pages (success criteria, risks, timeline)
- References: separate page(s), not counted
Any proposal that spends more than half a page on motivation has failed to allocate space effectively. The research plan is the most important section — it demonstrates understanding of the field and a sound approach.

## What Makes a CS GRFP Proposal Fundable vs Unfundable
FUNDABLE (characteristics seen in accepted proposals):
- Opens with a concrete problem, not a trend statement
- Names the gap with specific paper citations showing what prior work does NOT do
- Contains at least one explicit testable hypothesis with a measurable threshold
- Method names a specific dataset (n=X), named metric, and named baseline
- Broader Impacts has explicit local-tier activities at a named institution with named groups
- Acknowledges at least one real risk with a concrete mitigation
- Every claim either cites a paper or is labeled as the proposal's own hypothesis

UNFUNDABLE (characteristics seen in rejected proposals):
- Opens with a broad field statement ("AI is transforming software engineering")
- Claims novelty without naming what prior papers do
- Method says "we will use machine learning" without specifying the model, data, or evaluation
- Broader Impacts only mentions "publishing papers" and "attending conferences"
- No success criteria stated — unclear what "working" would mean
- Feasibility not addressed — leaves reviewers wondering if the data or participants are accessible

## GRFP Reviewer Red Flags — Exact Phrases That Trigger Skepticism
These phrases appear in reviewer comments on rejected proposals:
- "novel approach" without specifying what is novel
- "we will leverage [technique]" without explaining the mechanism
- "has the potential to" — signals the claim is not grounded in evidence
- "open-source projects" as the dataset — too vague, lacks reproducibility
- "standard metrics" — reviewers need specific metric names
- "future work" — if the proposal relies on future work that isn't planned, it isn't ready
- "preliminary results suggest" — without a link to actual data, this is not credible
